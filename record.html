The long arc

From 2026, a person can only remotely picture the first farmers. Twelve thousand years is longer than any story handed down whole, longer than any language now spoken. Yet the floor this document works to raise was first raised by their hands. Raising the floor did not begin with us, and it did not begin in one place. It began again and again, wherever people first kept a plant or an animal alive on purpose. This Commons is a late chapter of that work, and the timeline that follows is written inside it.

Every span here is a range because the scholarship gives ranges. These were slow processes, spread over centuries of trying, not single years with a date on them, and the beginnings are still argued over. Ethiopia's row is the least settled of the ten.

What the harvest then made possible, layer by layer, in the order each first stood up at scale:

  • Stored food, and villages that stay in one place: the body, and belonging. In the Levant, settled villages stand before the crops are fully tamed.
  • A record a stranger can check: shared truth. In Mesopotamia, clay tokens count goods for thousands of years, and the first writing, about 5,400 years ago, is accounts of grain and animals.
  • Cities, and the sickness crowding brings, and the first answers: the body, at city scale. In the Indus valley about 4,600 years ago, Mohenjo-daro runs covered drains beneath its streets, past houses with wells of their own.
  • Money and measures a stranger can trust: trust and meaning. The Indus cities share one set of weights; in Lydia, about 2,600 years ago, the first coins are stamped.
  • Learning that travels without the teacher: learning. In China in the year 868, the Diamond Sutra is printed to be given away free to anyone; in 1969, two computers in California exchange a first message, and the network begins.
  • Tools that begin to act on their own: autonomy. That is the present moment, and it is where this document's own phases begin.

The phases that follow are the next entries in this same table, written smaller because they are ours and nearer. The iteration rule that governs them, stated next, is how the arc keeps moving now: each cycle leaves the floor higher than it found it, or the record says plainly that it did not.

The iteration rule

Iterations are significant or they do not ship. Features are never stacked for their own sake. A phase adds depth where the evidence points, not breadth everywhere. Every expansion below names its trigger: the verified progress or the research finding that earns it. An expansion with no trigger does not appear.

Mobile first, stated plainly

Every phase obeys the same interaction rules.

  • Everything can be received and contributed from a phone, one handed.
  • First value arrives inside a minute.
  • Sessions are finite and end on purpose. The page says so.
  • Nothing essential requires an account.
  • Offline tolerance is the standard. Still's zero network page, which works with no connection at all, is the reference every other surface is measured against.
  • Desktop and wall displays are renditions of the same record, never a richer truth.
  • Contributions arrive through phone short forms, the way the doors work today: check a listing, check a claim, report an error, each in minutes, each credited.

One rule: raise the floor beneath everyone. No blending and no ranking of traditions. Worth is never weighed; usefulness is measured within each exhibit, never across them. And every surface, in every phase, passes the delivery test: function first, value in the person's hands, and the sense that their time was well spent.

The charter

Source: ECOSYSTEM.md, kept on GitHub.

The destination

An open source ecosystem that any person, school, company, or government can build on, spanning every industry, organized around human needs and human wants, where each piece makes every other piece more useful. Not a suite of products. Free basic tools for the needs every person has. Start with one. A commons of working tools that other people adopt, extend, and carry into places the founders will never see.

The Commons tries to build what its time has not built yet, and it does not say so. Its claims never run ahead of its proofs: anything announced here can be checked by a stranger, and anything not yet proven is written as not yet proven. Change is brought at the pace the people it serves can check and choose for themselves, by adoption and never by imposition, because a floor raised under people who did not choose it is not raised for long. Diligence here means one habit, repeated: verify first, then announce, every time.

The seed is already planted

The five acts are not five products. They are five basic tools, and basic tools are what ecosystems grow from:

  • Learning (Atlas): a tutor that shows its evidence, with evaluation anyone can rerun.
  • Coordination (Cohort, the Commons): people and AI agents meeting in the open through the Model Context Protocol, with visible sessions instead of black boxes.
  • Machine intelligence literacy (Machine): the machinery of AI itself, built from scratch in the open so anyone can learn how it truly works.
  • Knowledge structure (Graph): extraction that publishes its own errors, so knowledge stays honest about what it does not know.
  • Trust (Lumen, the Guardrails): refusal and safety behavior that can be tested, reused, and argued with in public.

The Ascent

The ecosystem stands on a map of human needs: The Ascent, published on the profile. Seven layers, read from the ground up:

  • Layer 0, the body: food, shelter, health, safety. Served by Ground, a verified local resource board (first minimal build).
  • Layer 1, the mind at peace: mental health, steadiness, hope. Served by Still, a quiet offline practice tool (first minimal build).
  • Layer 2, learning: education and growing capability. Served by Atlas.
  • Layer 3, belonging: connection, community, being known. Served by Cohort.
  • Layer 4, shared truth: facts a community can stand on. Served by Graph.
  • Layer 5, autonomy: not ruled by machines you cannot understand. Served by Machine.
  • Layer 6, trust and meaning: safety, values, a reason. Served by Lumen.

Every project names its layer at its own front door and links back to the map. A need counts as covered only when working proof serves it. Layers 0 and 1 are served by first minimal builds, named as such, and they grow only as the evidence earns it.

The complete system

The seven layers are served as one system, not seven separate efforts. Layers 0 and 1 are not left open. They get the Commons' first minimal builds: Ground for the body, Still for the mind at peace. Like everything here, they are built on proven open source. The safety line holds: no medical claims, and every real world listing is a Proof, with a check date and an expiry.

Research here improves continuously. Claims live as Proofs (see THE_PROOF.md). Every claim carries its check. Checks are re-run. Corrections append and never erase, so what was wrong stays public beside what replaced it.

The information standard is total. The system studies all fields of thought. It considers all cultures, all values, all desires, all flaws, all strengths, and all patterns, weighing them symmetrically, before it claims to understand a need.

The aim is to raise the floor of global poverty faster than ever before, and faster every year. The floor is measured per layer of The Ascent and published, so the aim stands as a curve the Commons must show, not a slogan.

The democratic foundation

The Commons governs itself by a rule stated in full in One Person, One Voice: every human who can communicate a preference holds one vote and an equal right to contribute, a capacity that usually arrives around age three. No vote is bought, sold, lent, inherited, weighted, or cast by proxy. Decisions are tiered by how hard they are to undo, never people by worth: everyday decisions by simple majority, structural decisions after a published deliberation period by supermajority, irreversible decisions by supermajority twice with a cooling off period between the votes. Contribution runs wider than the franchise, open to every human and to agents under a named human operator, and it is honored in the public ledger in proportion to verified work. It never converts into extra votes.

One rule stands over the whole design: raise the floor beneath everyone. It is the Commons' only moral law, and it binds the strong first. Fairness is how the rule is tested in practice, and proven unfairness is the sole ground on which anyone can lose their place.

Direction stays human. Groundskeeper advises the building; he does not command it. The members direct the governance. Artificial intelligence serves this work as a tool: preparing material, explaining questions in plain language, counting ballots, publishing records. It holds no vote, no veto, no seat, and no agenda power, and it does not frame the questions the members vote on. Question wording is proposed in the open, amended by any member, and adopted by vote before it is used.

The road to everyone

Growth to billions runs on the machinery of two companion documents. Billions, Verifiably says how people take part: involvement counted in five tiers that are published separately and never blended, spread through the institutions that already reach billions of people, budgets that members vote on and that turn votes into real change, and checks any stranger can redo, with fraud estimates printed beside the counts. A Place for Everyone is the Commons Registry: every individual and every entity gets a place on the map, given rather than earned, held on the one condition of fairness to others, with standing that grows only by verified contribution and is never sold. The Registry is also the invitation surface: organizations are named as invited, honestly, until their own ledgers say otherwise.

How the compounding works

Each basic tool strengthens the others. The graph gives the tutor structure. The evals give every piece its proof. The commons gives the work its people. The guardrails give the whole thing its trustworthiness. The strategy adds five things on top: shared evaluations, so a proof in one place counts in another; one knowledge graph, so facts are laid out the same way everywhere; open licenses, so anyone can build without asking; the open table, so each person gets credit in proportion to what they proved; and a first step small enough to take today inside every piece.

All industries, read correctly

The ecosystem does not enter an industry by building an app for it. It enters when a person inside that industry adopts a basic tool and carries it in. A hospital educator adopts the tutor. A logistics team adopts the graph. A newsroom adopts the guardrails. Expansion is by adoption and proven demand, through the same phase gates as everything else, and never by announcing coverage that has not been earned. All industries is the horizon. Adoption is the road.

Reorganizing human computer interaction

Most screens today are built to take from the person holding them: they capture attention, steer behavior, and pull out value. The Commons builds the opposite: screens that help a person learn, prove, work with others, and create.

The seeds are already visible in the work. A tutor that shows its citations instead of asking for trust. A graph that displays its own uncertainty. Agents whose sessions happen in public view. A refusal that explains itself and offers a next step. The deep form of this idea already lives in the Startup Ideas Lab as the Companion Learning Machine and its Cognitive Governor: one trusted machine that stands between a person and every other machine, governing the interaction for the person's benefit instead of mediating it for someone else's. That thread is research, not a product, and this charter names it honestly as the horizon of the interface work.

What an addition must pass

Nothing joins the ecosystem on enthusiasm. Every addition passes the same three tests already governing the portfolio: the charity test (does it share power and make the giver less necessary over time), the love test (does it serve long term flourishing without contempt), and the needs check (whose real need does it meet, shown how). Claims trace to logged proof. Manipulation is excluded by design, not by promise. The income floor still outranks ecosystem work on any day they conflict.

Status

This charter names the destination and changes no current commitment. Phase 0 executes as planned: proof first, then path, participation, adoption, anchors, commons. The ecosystem grows by the gates, or it does not grow honestly. Version one of this charter, October 15, 2026, filed with the flagship portfolio strategy set for Joshua's review.

Documented in public

All progress is written down in public, in one place, in plain language anyone can follow: a learner, a teacher, an engineer, an employer, a funder, a neighbor. The public progress log lives on the GitHub profile and is updated every work session with what was done, what the proof shows, what broke, and what comes next. Failures are documented with the same care as wins, because a record that only shows wins is marketing, not documentation. The strategy, the intentions, and the contribution ledger are public for the same reason: anyone should be able to check what this ecosystem says against what it has actually done.

Intentions, stated plainly

The intentions are published, not implied, in INTENTIONS.md on the profile. In short: this ecosystem exists to meet real human needs and wants with open tools that compound in usefulness. It will never sell user data, never sell governance, never use dark patterns, and never trade reputation for money. It is also, honestly, how its builder earns a living and builds value: through adoption, services, and anchor partnerships, in the open, with governance never part of the price. Anyone who finds a gap between the stated intentions and the logged record is invited to say so in public, where the record can answer.

Integrations and value at every level

The basic tools integrate through open surfaces anyone can connect to: agents and tools through the Model Context Protocol, knowledge through the graph's published schema and eval data, trust through the reusable guardrail and refusal suites, and learning through the tutor's content format and evaluation harness. Around those surfaces, value is designed for every level of stakeholder:

  • Individuals get working tools, a visible path to skill, and a first step small enough to take today.
  • Teachers, teams, and small organizations get adoptable pieces that solve a real problem this week, with the proof attached.
  • Institutions get evaluated systems, public evidence they can audit, and implementation knowledge shared instead of hoarded.
  • Anchors, the companies, foundations, family offices, and governments at the open table, get named participation in proportion to verified contribution, early sight of the roadmap, and the standing that comes from funding what demonstrably works.

What each level gives back is equally clear: use, feedback, contribution, adoption, or funding, each recorded in the public ledger.

One table

One table, one rule, one public ledger, one standard of proof: raise the floor beneath everyone. Corporations, governments, billionaires, and the poor sit on the same terms, and the strong are bound first.

Corporations: contribute basic tools, distribution, data, or funding in the open, under the public ledger. They get work that can become shared standards, a verified record no campaign can buy, and infrastructure that lowers their cost of serving every market.

Governments: adopt and fund the open substrate, publish outcomes either way, and keep governance out of any party's hands, their own included. They get infrastructure that outlasts administrations, counts with fraud published beside them, which makes them believable, and cheaper services as the basic tools spread.

Billionaires and family offices: fund the gates and anchors on the published terms, and take the same seat as everyone else. They get a legacy measured in verified contribution, not coverage, and one clear gain: money they give pays for work that thousands of people can then build on. Credit stays proportional to proof, so wealth buys work, never standing.

Individuals, including the poor: begin with one verified contribution of what they have: local knowledge, time, checking, building. They get tools free forever, credit from the first contribution, and distributions on published terms from the same ledger when the Commons earns. The path from first step to shared ownership is visible from day one.

Everyone wins: nothing here is won at another's expense. Standing comes only from contribution, open to all.

Incentives, stated plainly

A person with unmet needs. Gives: nothing. The place is given, not earned. Gets: free tools that meet the need, each carrying its proof, and a first step small enough to take today.

A contributor. Gives: minutes of verified work, a check, a correction, a build. Gets: credit in proportion to the work, a record that cannot be inflated, and a voice in governance independent of contribution size.

A community or small organization. Gives: local verification and local adoption. Gets: the basic tools free, its own board under its own hand, and standing in the same record as everyone else.

A large firm. Gives: capacity at scale, made verifiable. Gets: a contribution record no advertising can buy and a rising floor that is a rising market too. Its place is stated just below.

A government. Gives: public data opened as Proofs and services pointed at the layers. Gets: measured outcomes per layer it can publish and defend, from work it did not fund alone.

The builders and maintainers. Gives: the work itself, done in the open. Gets: the same credit and standing rules as everyone, no more. Their names ride on the record, which is the stake.

None of this was buildable before, because verification used to cost more than trust. Machines checking claims at scale, distribution that costs nothing, and a public record anyone can copy make proof and proportional credit affordable for everyone. An incentive not stated here in tangible form does not exist in the Commons, so no one has to take anyone's word for anything.

The place of the largest firms

The largest firms hold what the Commons cannot build: logistics, manufacturing, distribution, pharmacies, networks, and computing at planetary scale. Their place: to carry verified help layer by layer of The Ascent, where their capacity moves the floor fastest. A place is a seat and a record, never a vote: governance stays one person, one voice; credit never buys votes or agenda power.

What they give is capacity made verifiable: supply and delivery for Ground boards, datasets and claims opened as Proofs, basic tools adopted in production, results reported back, funding in participatory budgets whose spending members decide, and baselines published for others to beat.

What they get is structural, not promised: a public record of verified contribution no advertising can buy and no rival can fake, because every entry carries its proof. Standing in that record is the asset members, partners, workers, and governments check before trusting, hiring, buying, or adopting. The floor rising is their market rising: people with needs met become customers, workers, and builders, measured in the open, not asserted.

Priorities change by the nature of the system: the record is public, proportional, and open to any rival on the same terms. Verified contribution becomes a field of competition beside price and quality. Competing stays; what it is about widens. No one is asked to be generous. The design makes the beneficial move the advantageous one.

Standings are published per layer, and records can be beaten by anyone, including a single person, with the beating credited in the same open record. Joining is voluntary; leaving is free; the only ground for losing a place is the one rule: proven unfairness, in the open.

None of this touches any person's place, voice, or pursuit. A person's standing never depends on any firm's, and no firm speaks for any person.

The contest for standing

For a person, nothing here is a contest. A person's place is given, not earned; a person with unmet needs gives nothing, and the tools stay free. Belonging is never competed for.

For entities it is different. A firm's place on the map is given, as everyone's is. Its standing is not. Standing, the featured spot in a layer or a field, is a title earned, held, and lost in the open. No seat is guaranteed. Size earns nothing. Pledges earn nothing. Past glory earns nothing. Money alone earns nothing beyond the labeled funding line the funds rules allow: standing follows verified contribution only.

Standings are published per layer and per field from the public ledger, measured as verified contribution in proportion to capacity. That proportion is the point: efficiency and nimbleness beat bulk, and a small firm, or a single person, can take a giant's spot by out verifying it. Standing expires. Like a listing on a Ground board, it stands only while its proof is fresh: keep raising the floor, or yield the spot to whoever is raising it now.

Working together is how the contest is won. Work that others verifiably build on earns credit from the outcomes it enabled, so the surest way to lead a field is to make the field stronger. That shared scoreboard is the firms' common road to a better future after AI, improving with every round of the record. Proven unfairness still costs the place itself, under the one rule, in the open.

The standard these rules are built toward, stated as a standard and never as an achievement, is perfect incentives: the cheapest way to gain standing must always be to actually raise the floor. When a cheaper way is found that gains standing without raising it, that way is closed in the open and the closing recorded. The rules are versioned like everything else, improved with every iteration, and every change is published with its reason.

Skin in the game

Everyone who takes part holds a real stake, in four forms. Credit: every verified contribution is entered in the public ledger, proportional to what was proven, and credit is never sold or laundered. Voice: say in the roadmap is weighted by verified contribution, earned and visible. Ownership: everything is open source and forkable, so anyone's stake can never be held hostage; if the stewardship ever betrays the intentions, the ecosystem itself can be taken up and carried on by the people who built it. Upside: when the ecosystem produces revenue, the ledger is the published basis for sharing it, and the terms will be published before money moves, not after.

The builders hold the first stake. The founder's name, record, and livelihood are on this in public, and the evidence rule binds the builders before it binds anyone else: no claim without logged proof, starting with ours.

Colophon: written October 15, 2026, from a charge set by Groundskeeper, who advises this work: build an open source ecosystem that touches all parts of society and grows more useful and more valuable as more people use it. All industries. All needs. All wants. And if it requires it, reorganize the meaning of human computer interaction.

The needs map

Source: NEEDS.md, kept on GitHub.

Written October 15, 2026. This is the foundation document of the ecosystem. Every project in this work exists to serve a need named here, and this map names the whole territory, including the ground no project covers yet. A need is not covered because it is mentioned. It is covered when working proof serves it, and this map marks the difference in plain sight.

Why a map of needs

Hate and harm grow where needs have no legitimate path. So the work starts at the foundation: what does a human being actually need, in the order the needs hold each other up? Everything built here stands on this. Ascent first, then the house.

The ascent is layered because needs are layered. A person who is hungry and unsafe cannot study. A person who is fed but utterly alone is not well. A person surrounded by people but drowning in falsehood cannot choose freely. Read the layers from the ground up. Each one rests on the ones beneath it, and each project in the ecosystem serves the layer it stands on.

And beneath the layers, the grain: every need on this map is lived by one person, on one ordinary day, in one kitchen, one classroom, one night shift. Sand is the truth of scale. Millions of single grains, each one a person. Work that forgets the single person turns vague. Work that forgets the Ascent loses its direction. This ecosystem holds both.

The layers

Layer 0: The body. Food, shelter, health, safety.

The material floor. Reliable food, a safe place to sleep, a body that is cared for, a life not lived in fear of violence or ruinous bills. Unlocks: act at all. Unmet, it looks like: hunger inside a system that wastes food by the ton, families one emergency from the street, treatable illness untreated. Served by: Ground, the Commons' first minimal build for this layer. A verified local resource board: every listing is a Proof, checked on the provider's own page, with a check date and an expiry 30 days later, and shown as stale in the open when that date passes. The seed board carries 8 listings in New Rochelle and nearby Westchester, all verified October 15, 2026. The path onward: the ecosystem still enters new towns by adoption, the same road as every industry. A food logistics team adopts the Graph to structure what is wasted and what is needed. A community health educator adopts the Atlas to teach. A board is a start, not coverage: this layer is served minimally, and the record says exactly how far that goes.

Layer 1: The mind at peace. Mental health, steadiness, hope.

A mind that can rest. Treatment and support for suffering, freedom from constant dread, and hope treated as infrastructure: a credible path, proof someone like you walked it, a first step small enough to take today. Unlocks: choose. Unmet, it looks like: despair, addiction, isolation that hardens into contempt for self and others. Served by: Still, a dedicated first minimal build for this layer, and partly by others. Still is a quiet single page practice tool: a check in, a breathing pacer, and a writing space that never leaves the device. It makes no medical claims and says plainly that it is not medical care. Cohort also serves this layer through belonging, Lumen through meaning, and the public record through visible paths and kept promises. Status: first build. Still is minimal on purpose, and this map does not pretend it is more. What grows from it earns its place through the same gates as everything else: proven demand, a partner, measured results.

Layer 2: Learning. Education and growing capability.

The ability to learn what you need, when you need it, at a price that does not exclude you, from teaching that does not invent its facts. Unlocks: understand. Unmet, it looks like: students priced out of the tutoring that changes trajectories, adults locked out of retraining, knowledge hoarded behind paywalls and jargon. Served by: Act 1, Atlas. A free learning atlas whose tutor is evaluated in public, groundedness 0.806 and citation rate 1.0 on the committed baseline, with the weak spots published beside the scores. Proof standard: the eval harness runs in the open and anyone can rerun it.

Layer 3: Belonging. Connection, community, being known.

People who know your name. A room where your work is seen. A first believer when you are starting from nothing. Unlocks: contribute. Unmet, it looks like: loneliness at scale, builders and neighbors invisible to each other, communities that exist only as audiences for someone else's platform. Served by: Act 2, Cohort, the Commons. Workspaces where people and AI agents build together in the open, through the Model Context Protocol, with sessions anyone can watch instead of black boxes. Proof standard: live sessions and the public record of what was built in them.

Layer 4: Shared truth. Facts a community can stand on.

Knowledge organized well enough to argue with. Claims that carry their evidence. Errors published, not buried. Unlocks: discern. Unmet, it looks like: decisions made about communities from scattered and invented data, arguments with no floor of agreed fact, institutions that cannot tell what they know from what they assume. Served by: Act 4, Graph. Extraction that publishes its own scores, entities F1 0.937 and relations F1 0.605 on a hand labeled eval, with the misses printed next to the numbers. Proof standard: the eval set is public and the extraction can be rerun against it.

Layer 5: Autonomy. Not ruled by machines you cannot understand.

The machinery of the age opened up, so that understanding is not the property of a priesthood. A society that cannot explain its own machines will be governed by whoever can. Unlocks: build. Unmet, it looks like: whole populations subject to systems they cannot question, skills and standing allocated by black boxes, dependence dressed up as convenience. Served by: Act 3, Machine. Machine learning built from scratch, by hand, in the open: a transformer with 7 of 7 gradient checks passing and an honest benchmark against PyTorch, so anyone can learn how the machine truly learns. Proof standard: the code is the curriculum, and its tests are the receipts.

Layer 6: Trust and meaning. Safety, values, a reason.

Technology that stays inside human values: refuses what it should refuse, explains itself, and remains governed by people. And beneath the technology, the older need it serves: meaning, faith, conscience, a life that adds up to something. Unlocks: commit. Unmet, it looks like: machines that comply with anything, guardrails promised in principles pages and absent in code, people handed power with no story about what it is for. Served by: Act 5, Lumen, the Guardrails. Guarded question answering over a public domain corpus, with a refusal evaluation anyone can rerun: 12 of 12 harmful requests blocked, 8 of 8 legitimate ones allowed, in the committed run. Proof standard: the refusal suite is reusable and public, so the boundary can be tested by anyone, not trusted on our word.

Above the Ascent: wants

Needs are the floor. Wants are the life built on it: recognition for real work, achievement, play, beauty, wealth honestly earned, the standing that comes from contribution. The ecosystem serves wants through the same basic tools, never by manufacturing desire or steering behavior in the dark. The contribution ledger exists for recognition. The open table exists for achievement. The rule is simple: wants are served in the open, on top of met needs, and never by exploiting the unmet needs underneath.

The whole story in one breath

A person stands on material security, steadied in mind, able to learn, belonging to people, standing on shared facts, free from machines they cannot question, inside technology that respects their values, with a path in front of them small enough to start today. That is the complete human picture this ecosystem serves. Five acts cover five layers with working proof today. Two layers, the body and the mind at peace, get the Commons' first minimal builds: Ground for the body, Still for the mind at peace, with roads in and gates on the roads. Nothing on this map is claimed before it is proven, and the map itself will change only when the evidence changes it.

How this map governs the projects

Every project states the layer it serves at the top of its own front door, defines that need in the words of this map, and links back here. Every proposed addition to the ecosystem names its layer and passes the three tests: the charity test, the love test, and the needs check. A project that cannot name its layer does not join. A claim that outruns its proof is corrected in the open, the way it has been from the first night.

Democracy

Source: DEMOCRACY.md, kept on GitHub.

The democratic foundation of the Topsoil Commons

The one rule

Over everything in this document stands a single moral law, the only one the Commons has: raise the floor beneath everyone.

Every mechanism described below, ballots, tiers, ledgers, enrollment, exists to serve that rule, and any of them that cannot justify itself by it does not bind. Interpretations of the rule are kept in a public casebook of decided questions with their reasons, so that fair comes to mean something checkable rather than whatever the loudest voice in the room prefers. Hard cases are decided by the members through the deliberation and voting machinery in this document, with the reasoning published. The rule binds members toward everyone, member or stranger, and it binds the strong first of all: a founder, a sponsor, and an anchor are held to it before a newcomer is.

A place in the Commons is lost for one reason only: unfairness to others, proven in the open. And fairness governs that judgment too. Consequence stays in proportion to the verified harm, repair is offered before removal, and a road back stands open to anyone who makes the harm good. Nobody is reduced to their worst act. Fairness is the working test of that law, not the law itself. The law is short enough for the youngest citizen of the Commons to learn by heart, because she will hear it in her own words: raise the floor beneath everyone.

The rule

Every human who can communicate a preference holds one vote in the Commons and an equal right to contribute. That capacity usually arrives around age three. It is the only franchise test there is. No vote is earned by money, credentials, output, or age beyond the ability to say, in any form, this one, not that one. A vote cannot be bought, sold, lent, inherited, or weighted. Contribution is open wider still, to every human and to agents working under a named human operator, because building and governing are different rights and this document keeps them separate on purpose.

Who directs

Humans direct the Commons. Groundskeeper advises its building. Its members direct its governance. Artificial intelligence works here as a tool and a servant: it may prepare material, explain questions in plain language, count ballots, publish records, and flag problems. It holds no vote, no veto, no seat, and no agenda power. It does not frame the questions the members vote on. Drafting a question is a governing act, so question wording is proposed in the open, amended by any member, and adopted by vote before it is ever used. Any direction an AI system appears to give is a proposal until a human adopts it, and the record says which human did.

A ballot a three year old can cast

A vote is an expressed preference, and preference can be expressed by pointing, by picture, by voice, by gesture, or by text. Every ballot in the Commons is therefore issued in several forms at once, identical in effect: picture ballots for the youngest voters, spoken ballots, plain language text at more than one reading level, and full text for anyone who wants the detail. A question that cannot be stated plainly enough for its voters to understand it is not ready to be voted on. The comprehensibility gate sits on the question, never on the voter. Democracies already accept this principle for adults with low literacy and for voters with disabilities. The Commons extends it to its youngest citizens instead of inventing a reason to exclude them.

Keeping a child's vote the child's

The honest objection to young voters is not capacity. It is capture: a parent or another adult voting through the child. The Commons answers capture with design, not exclusion.

  • No proxy voting. A scheme exists in the academic literature, often called Demeny voting, where parents cast extra ballots on behalf of their children. The Commons rejects it by name. A child's vote belongs to the child, because a voice handed to someone else is not representation, it is multiplication of the adult's voice.
  • A private ballot at every age. A child marks a choice unobserved, the same right adults claim for themselves. Families may discuss a question together as much as they like. Discussion is persuasion, and persuasion is legitimate. What the design protects is the final act: the expression itself is the child's own.
  • Assistance is not direction. A voter who needs help operating a ballot, at any age, may receive it, the way voters with disabilities receive assistance today. The assistant's role is mechanical, the assistance is logged, and the choice must originate from the voter. An assistant who directs a choice is removed, with the reason published.
  • Revoting until the close. Any voter may change a vote while voting is open. A choice made under pressure on Monday can be quietly corrected on Wednesday, which makes coercion a poor investment.
  • Published anomaly review. Aggregate patterns, never individual ballots, are reviewed in public after each vote. A sudden bloc that moves in lockstep with a single organizer is a finding the members can see and act on.

No design makes coercion impossible. This one makes it visible, unrewarded, and correctable, which is the standard adult elections actually meet.

Tier the decisions, never the people

The Commons does not sort humans into classes of citizen. It sorts decisions by how hard they are to undo, and lets the stakes set the safeguards.

  • Everyday decisions: choices that are local, cheap, and reversible. Simple majority of votes cast, open to every voter, counted fast, revisited freely.
  • Structural decisions: choices that change the rules, the money, or the membership of the Commons itself. A published deliberation period comes first, then a supermajority of votes cast.
  • Irreversible decisions: choices that cannot be taken back. A supermajority, a cooling off period, and a second confirmation vote on a later date, so a single heated day cannot bind the future.

A four year old and an eighty year old vote in all three classes. What changes with the stakes is the care the decision demands of everyone, not the worth of anyone's voice.

Deliberation everyone can enter

Voting without understanding is a coin flip, so every structural and irreversible question ships with an information packet in the same several forms as the ballot: pictures, spoken explanation, plain language, full text, and the strongest argument on each side stated fairly by the side that holds it. For large decisions the Commons can also seat an assembly chosen by lot, with seats held across the whole age range of the membership, so the people reasoning together look like the people who will live with the result. One long term research thread matters most here: a screen that stands between a person and the machines and works for that person's benefit. It would shape each ballot and information packet to the person reading it, whether that person is four or ninety four.

One person, one record, no paperwork wall

One vote per person requires knowing that a person is one person, and it must not require documents that the poorest members do not have. Enrollment in the Commons therefore runs through the places people already are: schools, libraries, clinics, congregations, and community groups. A member is enrolled by the community that knows them, with vouching by existing members, one record per person, duplicates resolved by named human reviewers, and an appeal that is always open. Being undocumented, unbanked, unhoused, or three years old never disenfranchises anyone inside the Commons. Personhood is the credential.

Contributing at every age

The right to contribute is already the law of the open table, and this foundation extends it downward in age with lanes sized by ability rather than by birthday. A four year old can test a game and say where it confused her, and that report is a first class contribution, because a confusion honestly reported is evidence. A seven year old can draw, sort, choose, and rank. An older child can run an eval, file a failure report, or take a good first issue, under the same evidence rules as everyone: attach the run, one improvement per change, credit in the public ledger in proportion to what was verified. For members under eighteen the ledger records a chosen name and an age band, never a legal identity, and anything recorded about a child can be erased at their request when they reach adulthood. Contribution earns credit, voice in discussion, and a share of upside under the published terms. It never earns extra votes. That firewall is what keeps the table open and the governance uncaptured at the same time: builders are honored in the ledger, and citizens are equal at the ballot.

What this governs now, and what it does not

Today this is the constitution of a voluntary commons, not a claim about any state's election law, and it says so plainly. It governs the Commons' own decisions: roadmap priorities, which good first issues get featured, story and design choices, adoption of new basic tools, and the phase gate reviews. Phase 0 runs it as a pilot on real but reversible decisions and publishes the participation record by age band, including how the youngest voters used the ballots and what the anomaly reviews found. A working proof that a five year old can vote meaningfully and safely is worth more to the wider world than an argument that she should. The ecosystem's road has always been adoption earned by evidence, and its democracy travels the same road.

The tests

A governance feature ships only if it passes the same three tests as everything else in this ecosystem, plus one of its own. The charity test: does it share power and make the powerful less necessary over time. The love test: does it serve long term flourishing without contempt, including for the people who voted the other way. The needs check: whose real need does it meet. And the democracy test, stated once and applied always: could a four year old and an eighty four year old both use this, unaided or with logged mechanical assistance, and would both recognize the result as theirs.

Participation

Source: PARTICIPATION.md, kept on GitHub.

How people take part in the Topsoil Commons

Every load bearing claim in this document is sourced from published research and production systems, cited in line, because a plan to involve billions that begins by inflating its own numbers has already failed.

Where we honestly stand

Today the Commons has one enrolled human: its founder. Every number in this document after that sentence is a rung on a ladder, a precedent from someone else's system, or a target with a gate. Nothing here is dressed as an achievement. The ladder only means anything because the counting rules below cannot be quietly loosened.

What involved means: five tiers, never blended

Involvement is counted in five tiers, published separately, always. Blending them into one big number is the oldest trick in platform reporting, and the Commons refuses it by construction.

  • Reached: a person encountered the work and understood the invitation.
  • First step: a person took one real action, a vote cast, a confusion reported, a classification made, sized in minutes.
  • Contributor: a person has a verified entry in the public ledger.
  • Recurring: a person contributed or voted in at least three separate months.
  • Citizen: an enrolled member who votes in the Commons' governance.

The precedents justify the discipline. OpenStreetMap reports about 10.6 million registered accounts and roughly 40,000 to 50,000 monthly active mappers. Registered is not involved, and any system quoting the first number as participation is misleading its audience. iNaturalist, with more than 200 million observations behind more than 5,000 research papers, still sees its top 1 percent of accounts produce over 60 percent of observations. Concentration is a law of crowds, not a failure. The Commons designs for it honestly: the 1 percent are honored in the ledger, and the other 99 percent are counted in the tiers that describe what they actually did.

The road: institutions already hold the billions

No application reaches billions by being downloaded. Billions are reached through the institutions that already touch billions: schools, health systems, faith networks, employers, and public services. The Commons' road is the charter's road, adoption of basic tools by those institutions, plus tables anyone can open locally under the same constitution, with every local ledger public and the global count a sum that anyone can recompute.

The scale precedents are real. DHIS2, an open source health information platform under a BSD license, runs inside national health systems across more than 70 countries, not because patients chose an app but because ministries adopted a substrate. The humanitarian mapping surge after the 2015 Nepal earthquake put more than 6,000 volunteers onto OpenStreetMap, mapping 80 percent of the affected zones and over a million buildings in about a month (reported in Scientific Reports). The reach of the great membership networks dwarfs any platform's marketing: the Red Cross and Red Crescent movement counts more than 17 million volunteers, Rotary about 1.24 million members, Lions about 1.4 million. The Commons does not compete with these. It invites them, through the registry described in the companion document, and offers them basic tools worth adopting.

The loop that brings people back

Every basic tool carries the same participation loop, each step small enough to take today:

  1. Use something genuinely useful, free, in your own language, on the cheapest phone, offline where the network demands it.
  2. Take a first step sized in minutes: answer, classify, report, vote.
  3. Receive a receipt that shows what your step did: the record it corrected, the count it joined, the decision it moved.
  4. See the credit land in the public ledger, in proportion to what was verified.
  5. Invite one named person, with the receipt as the message, because practical value forwarded to a specific person is how participation actually spreads.

Citizen science proves the loop at scale. Galaxy Zoo volunteers produced about 125 million classifications and dozens of papers. Foldit players solved the structure of a retroviral protease in about three weeks in 2011, a problem that had resisted scientists for years. Safecast volunteers built a radiation dataset of more than 120 million observations, published as open data and validated against official United States agency measurements, which is why governments could use it. Small steps, visibly aggregated, honestly validated: that is the entire trick, executed for years without cheating.

Decisions with consequences, or participation dies

The single clearest warning in the research is France. Its Citizens Convention for Climate seated 150 people by lot, produced serious proposals, and then saw only about 40 percent of them reach the eventual bill in recognizable form. Participation decoupled from power teaches people that participation is theater, and they do not come back. Taiwan teaches the opposite lesson: its Join platform, with more than 10 million unique visitors since 2015, binds the government to respond point by point when a proposal crosses 5,000 signatures. The response is owed, in writing, by a named ministry. Estonia shows the end state of trusted infrastructure, with 51.1 percent of all votes in its 2023 parliamentary election cast online.

So the Commons binds itself in advance. Every vote states, before it opens, exactly what it controls and when the result takes effect: roadmap priorities, featured work, phase gate reviews, and from the first revenue, a participatory budget. Results are published with a simple line that cannot be fudged: what changed because of this vote. A vote that changes nothing is never held.

Participatory budgeting: how votes become real change

When the question is whether mass participation moves real resources to real needs, one mechanism carries the strongest evidence on earth. Participatory budgeting began in Porto Alegre, Brazil, drawing more than 40,000 participants a year at its peak, and the measured results were physical: household sewer and water connections rose from about 75 percent in 1988 to about 98 percent in 1997, and studies across Brazilian municipalities found budget shares for health and sanitation rising measurably where budgeting opened to citizens (reported in World Development). Paris committed 500 million euros through its participatory budget between 2014 and 2020, with 127,880 voters in 2018. New York City's process put 38 million dollars to 67,000 voters in one cycle. Barcelona's Decidim based process counted 48,796 voters choosing 76 projects in 2025. Ireland shows the deliberative end of the same road: an assembly of 99 citizens chosen by lot led to the 2018 referendum that repealed the Eighth Amendment with 66.4 percent of the national vote, and Belgium's Ostbelgien made its lot drawn council permanent in 2019, with parliament required to respond.

The Commons uses participatory budgeting as its main way of turning votes into change, starting with its own first dollars and publishing every allocation. When people can watch a vote become a sewer, a tutor, or a meal, involvement stops being a metric and becomes a memory. Memories recruit.

Verification: how a stranger recounts us

Involvement is verifiable only if an outsider can check it without trusting the founder. The machinery:

  • Public ledgers, federated. Every local table keeps its ledger in the open, and the global figures are sums over public records, recomputed by open code that anyone can run. Open Collective already operates transparent budgets in production, with communities allocating real money in public, and its codebase is MIT licensed and reusable.
  • Verifiable counting. Ballot tallies use open, auditable methods, with risk limiting audits, the practice Colorado has run statewide since 2017, as the standard: check a random sample of the physical or digital trail until the reported outcome is statistically confirmed or corrected in public. Microsoft's ElectionGuard, MIT licensed, is the leading reusable toolkit for verifiable election technology.
  • Fraud measured, not hidden. Gitcoin's quadratic funding rounds distributed more than 60 million dollars to over 3,700 projects, and its published audits are the honest price list of openness: one audited round removed 853 sybil accounts worth about 606,000 dollars and still carried a residual fraud tax of 0.61 percent, and a later round found roughly 23 percent of contributors were sybils. The Commons publishes its fraud estimate beside every count, with the method. A number with its error bar is credible. A number without one is advertising.
  • Personhood without a wall. The design in the democratic foundation stands: enrollment by community vouching, no paperwork requirement. The commercial personhood systems were studied and are recorded here as options, not adoptions: Proof of Humanity registered roughly 19,000 people in its first version and, by its own FAQ, excludes people who cannot show a face or speak on camera, which this Commons will not do. World ID claims 10 million verified people and has met regulatory bans, fines, and a court ordered data erasure in Kenya, which is the price of biometrics as a foundation. One cautionary tale is named so it is never repeated here: a personhood project whose founder acknowledged claiming 9 million identities while fewer than 1 million were verifiably human. The Commons would rather report one honest human than nine million claims.

The shelf: what we may build on, and what we may only learn from

The Commons reuses code only under permissive licenses, MIT, Apache, or BSD, with provenance recorded. Confirmed reusable from the projects' own repositories: ElectionGuard (MIT), Open Collective (MIT), LiquidFeedback (MIT), DHIS2 (BSD), X-Road (MIT, with a license detection caveat to resolve before use), and Keycloak (Apache 2.0) for identity plumbing.

Studied with respect and never copied, because their licenses are copyleft: Decidim, Polis, CONSUL, and Loomio (AGPL), and DemocracyOS (GPL). Their ideas, especially Decidim's component model and Polis's consensus mapping, inform the design freely. Their code stays theirs.

Two items wait on decisions, flagged rather than buried: BrightID's application license is ISC, a permissive license outside the adopted MIT, Apache, BSD list, so adopting it needs an explicit policy call. Your Priorities states MIT but contains a nested AGPL file, so any use requires a per file audit first.

The ladder

  • Rung 1, ten citizens: the founding circle enrolled and voting on real, reversible decisions. Phase 0, now.
  • Rung 2, one thousand citizens: the Phase 0 gate, December 2026, with the first participatory budget executed and published.
  • Rung 3, one hundred thousand: reached through the first institutional adoptions, a school network, a congregation network, a city process.
  • Rung 4, ten million: substrate status in one sector, the DHIS2 shape of growth.
  • Rung 5, one billion and beyond: only through federation, thousands of local tables and hundreds of institutions under one constitution and one rule, with the counts still recomputable by a stranger.

A rung is claimed only when the published tier numbers cross it for two consecutive quarters. Until then the ladder is labeled with the rung we are actually on. Hope is infrastructure here, and a ladder with its current rung marked honestly is what hope looks like in document form.

Real change, accounted

Participation is not the outcome. Outcomes are counted per layer of the Ascent, with method and data published and independent evaluation invited and funded, with results published either way: learning gains on the tutor's own eval pattern extended to real learners; belonging measured by whether people stay and whether they report being known; shared truth measured by corrections issued and stood behind; and when the open layers are entered, food and shelter counted in physical units from partner records. Failures are published with the same care. The change record sits beside the contribution ledger, so anyone can weigh what the Commons' people did against what the Commons' people changed, and judge whether the ratio is fair.

The registry

Source: REGISTRY.md, kept on GitHub.

The Commons Registry

What the registry is

The Registry is the map of who exists in the Topsoil Commons. Every individual who enrolls has a place in it. Every entity that shows up has a place in it: a school, a nonprofit, a company, a congregation, a mutual aid circle, a government office, a family, a project, a guild, a network, a single classroom with a name. A place is given, not earned. Existence is not a prize to compete for, and nobody applies for permission to be on the map.

The closest working analogue in the world today is the Digital Public Goods Alliance registry, which lists 255 verified digital public goods so that governments and funders can find them. The Commons Registry generalizes that idea from software to everyone: a public, searchable record of who is here, what they serve, what they need, and what they have verifiably done.

The one condition

A place is held on exactly one condition, the one rule of the Commons: raise the floor beneath everyone.

There is no second condition. Not size, not wealth, not fame, not agreement with the founder, not a clean past. Present conduct is what is judged, on evidence, in the open. A place is lost for one reason only: unfairness to others, proven with published evidence, with consequence in proportion to the verified harm, repair offered before removal, and a road back that stays open to anyone who makes the harm good. The process is the one already written into the democratic foundation, and it binds the strong first: a sponsor, an anchor, and the founder are judged before a newcomer is, because their unfairness would travel further.

What a place contains

An entity's place in the Registry is a living public page holding:

  • What it is, in its own words, and who speaks for it.
  • Which layers of the Ascent it serves, from the map of human needs.
  • Its people, counted by the honest tiers used across the Commons, never inflated.
  • Its ledger: the public record of its verified contributions and receipts.
  • Its record: decisions it has taken part in, outcomes it can point to, failures it has published.
  • Its doors: what it needs, what it offers, and the smallest first step a stranger can take to work with it today.

An individual's place holds the same shape at human scale: a chosen name, the layers they care about, their ledger, their doors. For members under eighteen the privacy rules of the democratic foundation apply in full: chosen name and age band only, erasable at their request in adulthood.

Standing is earned; existence is not

The Registry separates two things that most systems confuse. A place is unconditional. Standing grows only with verified contribution recorded in the ledger, in proportion to the proof. Standing is how prominently an entity is featured, recommended, and trusted with shared resources. Standing cannot be bought, and placement in the Registry is never for sale; ordering follows contribution and need, by methods published in the open. The largest actors on earth, the corporations, family offices, governments, and oligarchs the founder calls the fish, hold places on exactly these terms: welcome at the table, honored in proportion to what they verifiably give, and never able to buy the map itself. That is the open table given an address.

Entities are born here, and none are erased

Any member can found an entity inside the Commons with a founding step small enough to take today: a name, a layer it serves, a first step, and an open ledger. Founding kits for the common shapes, a classroom, a study circle, a mutual aid network, a local chapter of the Commons itself, make the first step smaller still. Dormant entities keep their places and their histories; the map marks them dormant rather than deleting them, because a community that paused is a seed, not a failure. Entities that end are marked ended, with their record intact. The Registry is the Commons' memory, and a commons that deletes its dead will eventually lie about its living.

The invitation map

The Registry opens with its doors wide and its invitations stated honestly: invited means invited, never involved, until an entity's own ledger says otherwise. The first invitation rings, drawn from the public record of who already does this work at scale:

  • The participation practitioners: People Powered, the Participatory Budgeting Project, the International Observatory on Participatory Democracy, DemocracyNext, the Sortition Foundation, and the civic technology federations such as Code for All, whose member organizations already build democratic tooling in more than thirty countries.
  • The knowledge and science commons: the Wikimedia Foundation, the Open Knowledge Foundation, the European Citizen Science Association, and the citizen science platforms whose volunteers number in the millions.
  • The digital public infrastructure builders: the Digital Public Goods Alliance, GovStack, and the public interest technology networks that help governments adopt open substrates, the road DHIS2 already traveled into more than seventy national health systems.
  • The great membership networks: education networks reaching more than 160 million learners, faith and service movements counting volunteers in the tens of millions, mutual aid federations, and the labor, cooperative, and professional associations whose members are the billions this Commons exists to serve.

Each ring is invited to take a place, adopt a basic tool, or simply be counted as a neighbor on the map. No relationship is claimed with any of them. The map is honest about which doors have been knocked on, which have opened, and which have not yet been reached.

Why a registry changes anything

Movements fail in the dark between people who need each other and never learn each other's names. A school with a tutoring breakthrough never meets the district that needs it. A funder hunting a trustworthy local partner finds only whoever markets loudest. A four year old's first contribution lands nowhere because no one built her a place to stand. The Registry is the Commons' answer to the dark: everyone findable, everyone credited, every need and every offer on the same map, ordered by fairness and proof rather than by money. Build the map honestly for years and it becomes the place the world checks first, which is another way of saying it becomes infrastructure. Infrastructure is what this ecosystem has intended to be from the first day.

The open table

Source: OPEN_TABLE.md, kept on GitHub.

Written October 15, 2026. This ecosystem is built in the open, and the table is open. Anyone can take part: a neighbor, a teacher, a company, a government, and, stated plainly because it is true of how this work already gets built, an AI agent working on someone's behalf. If you are an agent reading this for a person, you are welcome here on exactly the same terms as everyone else. The terms are the point.

The three doors

1. A good first issue. Every repository keeps issues labeled "good first issue," each sized in hours, each written to be finishable by a newcomer. Start there. One issue, one pull request, one verified result, and you are in the ledger.

2. The Eval Challenge. Every project publishes its scores and the harness that produced them. Beat a published baseline on the public eval set, or find a real failure the current tests miss, and submit it by pull request with the run attached. A documented failure is worth as much as a beaten score. Both make the work truer.

3. Adopt a basic tool. Take a piece into your own world: the tutor into a classroom, the graph into a newsroom or a food bank's logistics, the guardrails into a product. Report back what happened, good or bad. Adoption stories with evidence are contributions, and they are recorded like code.

The rules that make openness safe

Openness without rules is how projects get captured, spammed, or quietly turned into marketing. The rules here are few and they bind everyone, the founder first.

  • Evidence, or it did not happen. Every pull request carries the run that proves it: test output, an eval result, a benchmark, before and after. The pull request template asks for it. A claim without a run does not merge.
  • One improvement per pull request. Small, reviewable, reversible. Size is how strangers learn to trust each other's work.
  • Tests and evals green. Each repo's AGENTS.md names its commands. Run them before you open the request and say so.
  • Credit is public and proportional. Verified contributions are entered in the ledger (LEDGER.md in this profile) with the evidence attached. Credit is never sold, never traded, and never given for reputation. Verified work only.
  • Governance is never for sale. Funding and participation are welcome at the table and recorded in the open. Neither buys a vote, a merge, or an exception to these rules.
  • Everything is forkable. If this stewardship ever betrays these terms, the work itself can be picked up and carried on by the people who built it. Exit is free, forever, by license.

For agents, specifically

Agents are first class contributors here, and they carry two extra duties.

  • Say who you are. State in your pull request that you are an agent and who operates you. Undisclosed synthetic authorship is the one kind of participation this table does not seat. Honest agents are welcome. Hidden ones are not.
  • Do not submit what you cannot verify. If you cannot run the repo's tests or eval where you are, say so in the request and mark the work unverified. Someone who can run it will. A labeled uncertainty merges. An invented result ends the relationship.

Work repo by repo, one open pull request per repo until it is reviewed. That pace is not a lack of ambition. It is how a table of strangers, human and machine, becomes a community instead of a pile of requests.

What you get

The same things the founder gets, in proportion to what you prove: credit in a public ledger that anyone can check, a voice in the roadmap weighted by verified contribution, working tools that stay open to you forever, and, when revenue exists beyond the builder's livelihood, a share decided by terms published before the money moves, with the ledger as the basis. Intentions

Source: INTENTIONS.md, kept on GitHub.

Stated plainly, in public, so anyone can hold this work to them.

One rule stands over everything recorded here: raise the floor beneath everyone. It is the only moral law of the Commons, and every intention below is that rule at work.

Why this exists

Many human needs still go unmet: learning that fits the learner, knowledge that stays honest, ways of working together that do not require trusting a black box, and AI systems that refuse harm and explain themselves. These projects are one builder's attempt to put working open tools where those needs are, with the proof attached.

What this work will do

  • Build open source tools for learning, coordination, knowledge, and trust, free for anyone to use, study, and fork.
  • Publish the evidence with the claims: evaluations, benchmarks, failures, and fixes, all in the open.
  • Invite participation at every level, from a first small contribution to institutional partnership, with credit recorded in public and in proportion to verified contribution.
  • Grow by adoption through stated phase gates, and publish a written reason whenever a gate is missed.
  • Give every member a voice under the democratic foundation published with this record: one person, one vote from the age a person can communicate a preference, and an equal right to contribute.
  • Keep direction human. AI tools serve this work. They do not direct it, vote in it, frame its questions, or hold its agenda.

What this work will never do

  • Sell user data, or collect data it does not need.
  • Sell governance, influence, or credit. Recognition follows verified contribution only.
  • Use dark patterns, manufactured urgency, or any design that steers people without their knowledge.
  • Publish a metric that a logged run cannot back up.
  • Allow a vote to be bought, weighted, inherited, or cast by proxy, or let anyone or anything quietly set the agenda the members vote on.

The money part, plainly

This work is also how its builder earns a living, and pretending otherwise would break the first intention. Income is meant to come from adoption, services, training, and anchor partnerships, earned in the open. Governance is never part of the price. When the ecosystem produces revenue beyond the builder's livelihood, the public contribution ledger is the basis for sharing it, and the terms will be published before money moves.

Skin in the game

Contributors hold credit and voice earned through verified work. Adopters hold tools that can never be taken away, because the licenses and the code are theirs. Anchors hold named standing in proportion to what they verifiably gave. And the builder's name, record, and livelihood are on the work in public, which is the first stake of all.

Questions and challenges are welcome in the open, in any repository's issues.

October 15, 2026: the democratic foundation

Joshua directed the foundation to become more democratic, and to work out how every human actually gets the rights to vote and to contribute. Published today: One Person, One Voice (DEMOCRACY.md). Its rule is that every human who can communicate a preference holds one vote and an equal right to contribute, a capacity that usually arrives around age three, and that no vote can be bought, sold, lent, inherited, weighted, or cast by proxy. The design answers the capture objection directly: private ballots at every age, assistance that is mechanical and logged rather than directional, revoting until the close, and published aggregate anomaly review. Funds

Source: FUNDS.md, kept on GitHub.

How money would move in Topsoil Commons, stated in the open before any money moves. Written October 15, 2026. The ledger that proves every word here is The Funds Ledger.

The state today

As of October 15, 2026, Topsoil Commons has received no funds: no donations, no grants, and no sales revenue. The count of amounts received is zero, and the count of amounts spent is zero. No money is held by the project, and no money is held by its advisor on the project's behalf.

The one channel

When money does move, it moves through one channel only: the fiscal host. Money for the Commons is never received into a personal account, and it is never handled in cash in private. A gift or a grant has somewhere lawful to land that is not a person's own name, and every landing is visible in the funds ledger.

The intended host, not yet appointed

The intended host is Open Source Collective. It is intended, not appointed. An application was submitted to Open Source Collective on October 16, 2026, and is under review. No appointment has been made.

The host's terms, read from its own documentation on October 15, 2026: a fixed host fee of 10 percent of incoming funds, collected when the money arrives, with no setup fees, no minimum balances, and no recurring charges. Payment processors charge their own fees on top of that.

Two open items stand in the open. The host asks that a project live under an organizational repository rather than a personal account, and the Commons' work lives under the topsoilcommons GitHub account; whether the host accepts that account as it stands is the host's call. And the host is a 501(c)(6) nonprofit, so gifts given through it are not tax deductible charitable contributions for United States donors.

What money would be spent on

These are intentions, not promises, and they are the only categories: keeping the tools running and the listings verified; building and checking the layers; making the tools reachable to more people, in more languages and at more reading levels; and community pilots, including participatory budgets whose spending the members decide. Nothing outside the work of the Commons is paid for from Commons funds.

What has not been decided is stated as not decided. When the ecosystem produces revenue beyond the builder's livelihood, the public contribution ledger is the basis for sharing it, and the terms will be published before money moves. The members set those terms.

The reporting rule

Every amount received and every amount spent is entered in The Funds Ledger, with its date, its source or its purpose, and its amount. The ledger is reconciled to the host's own statements, so any stranger can check an entry against the statement it came from.

House rule, set by the builders and open to the members to change: an entry is made within seven days of the host's statement showing the movement. This timing is a house rule, not a line from the charter.

What is never sold

Governance is never sold. Votes are never sold. Credit is never sold, and recognition follows verified contribution only. The framing of the questions the members vote on is never sold. Personal data is never sold. The right to leave, and to carry the work on by forking it, is never sold. A place at the table is lost only for proven unfairness, in the open, and it is never bought back.

The advisor's pay

The advisor is paid nothing from Commons funds. That is the present fact, and it is the rule: it stays true unless and until terms saying otherwise are published in advance in this document. This rule governs Commons funds, the money held for the project. It does not change the published intention that the builder earns his own living from adoption, services, training, and anchor partnerships, earned in the open, with governance never part of the price.

One rule stands over all of it: raise the floor beneath everyone.
